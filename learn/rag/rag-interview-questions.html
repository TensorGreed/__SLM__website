    RAG interview questions, with answers you can check

    These are the questions that come up when someone is checking whether you have built a retrieval system rather than read about one: how documents are cut, how passages are scored, how you know retrieval works, and when RAG is the wrong tool. Each answer is short enough to say out loud, and each links to a page where the claim is measured with code you can run on a CPU, on the same 80 questions about IRS Publication 17.

    How to use this page

    Read the question, answer it aloud, then open the answer. Interviewers rarely want a definition alone; they want the trade-off and one thing you would measure. Where an answer quotes a number, it comes from a page in this track that ran the code on the same corpus: IRS Publication 17 (2025), cut into 2,170 chunks of up to 512 characters, with 80 frozen questions whose answers are verbatim spans of the publication. Those numbers are one document and one set of small models; treat them as an example of how to measure, not as constants.

    The questions run roughly in the order a pipeline does: what RAG is, cutting documents, retrieval, ranking, answers, evaluation, and the decisions around it.

    Questions

    1. What is RAG, and what problem does it solve?

    Retrieval-augmented generation retrieves passages relevant to a question from your documents and puts them in the model's prompt, so the answer comes from the passages rather than from what the model memorised. It solves missing, private or changing knowledge without training. On the what is RAG page a 1.5B model on CPU matched a short answer key for 8 of 47 keyed questions about IRS Publication 17 on its own, 30 with five passages from a plain embedding search and 41 with reranked passages.

    2. Walk me through a RAG pipeline from documents to answer.

    Offline: extract text, clean it, cut it into chunks, embed each chunk (and build a keyword index), store the vectors. Per question: embed the question, retrieve the top candidates by similarity and keywords, fuse and rerank them, put the best few in a prompt with instructions to answer only from them and cite, generate, and log what was retrieved. The pages on chunking, hybrid search and reranking each take one stage.

    3. How do you choose a chunk size?

    Start from the length of the thing that must stay whole (a sentence, a clause, a table row), check the embedding model's input limit in tokens, then compare two or three sizes on labelled questions at the same number of characters retrieved, not the same k. On Pub 17 at a 2,048-character budget, 256-character chunks found 68 of 80 answers, against 52 for 1,024-character ones and 44 for 128-character ones, where many answers no longer fit in a chunk (chunk size and overlap).

    4. Is chunk overlap worth it?

    Overlap keeps a fact that straddles a boundary whole in at least one chunk, but it repeats text, so a fixed prompt budget holds less new material. Measure it: on Pub 17, 25% overlap made more questions answerable at 128 characters (57 to 66) yet never improved recall by a significant margin at any size (chunk size and overlap). Add it when answers are being cut, not by default.

    5. Explain BM25. What do k1 and b do?

    BM25 scores a document by summing, over query terms, the term's inverse document frequency times a saturating function of how often it appears in the document, normalised by document length. k1 controls how fast repeated terms stop adding score; b controls how much long documents are penalised (0 none, 1 full). Defaults of k1 1.2 to 1.5 and b 0.75 are usually fine: on Pub 17 sixteen k1 and b settings moved top-5 recall only between 52 and 58 of 80, while dropping stop words moved it from 56 to 63 (p = 0.065, suggestive) (BM25).

    6. When does keyword search beat dense retrieval?

    When the question carries exact tokens that matter: form numbers, codes, names, rare terms. Dense models blur those into meaning. Dense wins on paraphrases and different wording. On Pub 17 they tied at top 5 (BM25 56, MiniLM 57, 12 against 11 discordant questions), but they found different questions, and their union at top 5 covered 68 (dense retrieval).

    7. How do you combine BM25 and dense results?

    Reciprocal rank fusion: each list contributes 1 / (k + rank) for every document, and you sort by the sum. It needs no score calibration, because it uses ranks only. On Pub 17 RRF found 67 of 80 at top 5, against 57 for dense and 56 for BM25, and the paired tests gave p = 0.013 against each. A tuned weighted sum of normalised scores did no better (hybrid search and RRF).

    8. What is the k in RRF, and does it matter?

    It damps the advantage of the very top ranks: with small k, first place dominates; with large k, ranks further down count nearly as much. The usual 60 comes from the original paper. On Pub 17 values from 1 to 200 moved top-5 recall between 67 and 70 of 80, which is within noise for 80 questions (hybrid search and RRF).

    9. Bi-encoder or cross-encoder: what is the difference, and why rerank?

    A bi-encoder embeds query and passage separately, so passage vectors are computed once and search is a fast vector lookup. A cross-encoder reads query and passage together and outputs one relevance score: more accurate, but it must run once per pair, so it only reorders a short candidate list. On Pub 17, reranking the hybrid top 30 with a small six-layer cross-encoder raised top-1 hits from 40 to 54 of 80 (19 gained, 5 lost, p = 0.007) and MRR from 0.637 to 0.768 (reranking).

    10. How many candidates should the reranker see?

    Enough that the right passage is usually in the pool, few enough to keep latency acceptable; the reranker can only reorder what it is given. Measure recall of the pool, then rerank quality as the pool grows. On Pub 17 gains flattened after a pool of 20: top-5 hits were 69 at 10 and 72 at 20, 30 and 50 (reranking).

    11. When does query rewriting help?

    Rewriting turns the user's question into a better search query, a keyword version or several variants fused together. It pays off most for conversational follow-ups and vague queries, and when the rewriter knows the corpus's vocabulary; it can also drop the one word that mattered. With a 1.5B rewriter on Pub 17, replacing the question lowered BM25 recall from 56 to 49 of 80, appending the rewrite raised it slightly (59, not significant), and multi-query fusion lowered dense recall from 57 to 49. Keep the original query in the mix and measure. (query rewriting)

    12. What is HyDE, and when would you use it?

    Hypothetical document embeddings: a language model writes a passage that would answer the question, and you embed that instead of the question, because a fake answer resembles real answers more than a question does. It helps most when the writer knows the domain. With a 1.5B writer on Pub 17, the passage alone found fewer answers (53 against 57 of 80 at top 5, and 19 against 36 at top 1), and averaging it with the question vector found 62, not significant, for one extra generation per query. (HyDE)

    13. How do you evaluate retrieval on its own?

    Label which passages answer each question (or which contain a known answer span), then score the ranked lists: hit@k (any relevant passage in the top k), recall@k (the share of relevant passages found), MRR (how high the first relevant one is) and nDCG (graded, position-discounted). On Pub 17, reranking raised MRR from 0.637 to 0.768 with a bootstrap interval of +0.060 to +0.205, while its hit@5 gain had an interval crossing zero (RAG evaluation metrics).

    14. Why might hit@5 and MRR disagree?

    hit@5 only asks whether the right passage is anywhere in the top five; MRR rewards putting it first. A reranker that moves answers from rank 4 to rank 1 changes MRR a lot and hit@5 not at all. If the generator reads all five passages, hit@k matters more; if it mostly uses the first, MRR does (RAG evaluation metrics).

    15. How do you know an improvement is real?

    Score both systems on the same questions and test the paired differences: McNemar's test for a yes/no outcome, a paired bootstrap for averages. Report the interval, not only the mean, and say "suggestive" when it crosses zero. With 80 questions a three-question gap is usually noise. The lift calculator does the arithmetic; the metrics page shows the bootstrap in code.

    16. What is faithfulness, and how is it different from correctness?

    Faithfulness asks whether every claim in the answer is supported by the passages the model was given; correctness asks whether it is true. A faithful answer built on a stale passage is wrong, and a correct answer from memory is unfaithful. Checkers (NLI models, LLM judges) need testing first: on 23 Pub 17 sentences, two small NLI models accepted only 8 and 13 of the true ones and still passed 4 and 5 copies with a changed number, while a 1.5B judge accepted all true ones and 3 changed ones. (faithfulness)

    17. How do you make a RAG system cite its sources?

    Number the passages in the prompt, ask for the passage id and a verbatim quote, and verify both in code: the id must exist and the quote must appear in that passage after normalising whitespace and curly quotes. A passing check proves where the words came from, not that they answer the question. On Pub 17 a 1.5B model cited a valid passage in 79 of 80 answers, cited the passage that held the answer in only 46 of the 72 where it was retrieved, and marked a quote in just 3. (citations and quoting)

    18. RAG or fine-tuning?

    RAG for facts from documents, fine-tuning for behaviour: format, style, a narrow task, or reading passages better. BrewSLM's published Pub 17 run scored a 3B model 0.17 alone, 0.73 with passages, and 0.02 after fine-tuning on 405 question-answer pairs; a later fine-tune that taught a 1.5B model to read retrieved passages did beat the same model reading them untrained, 0.688 against 0.570 for the published seed (+0.07 to +0.12 across three seeds). RAG also updates without training: after one digit was changed in 26 Pub 17 chunks and those were re-embedded, 11 of 23 answers gave the new number. (RAG vs fine-tuning)

    19. Why not put the whole document in a long context window?

    Because it often doesn't fit, and when it does every question pays for reading all of it. IRS Publication 17 is about 286,000 tokens, 8.7 times a 32,768-token window. Its first chapter alone was about 26,300 tokens per question against about 600 for five retrieved chunks, and the first such question took over 20 minutes on CPU; prefix caching helps only when every question shares the same document. On 11 questions the two scored 7 and 6, a tie. (long context vs RAG)

    20. What is parent-document retrieval?

    Search over small chunks, which embed precisely, and return the larger section each hit belongs to, so the generator gets context. It helps when answers need the surrounding text. On Pub 17's single-fact questions, finding 1,024-character parents through 256-character children beat searching the parents directly (62 against 52 of 80, p = 0.052), but eight plain 256-character chunks did better still (69) (parent-document retrieval).

    21. Pre-filtering or post-filtering on metadata?

    Pre-filtering searches only chunks that match the filter and always returns k results; post-filtering searches everything and drops non-matching hits, so it can return fewer, even none. Hard rules such as permissions must be pre-filters. Guessed filters are riskier: on Pub 17 the correct chapter filter raised dense recall from 57 to 68 of 80, but a router guessing the chapter from the question ended below no filter at all (metadata filters).

    22. Your RAG system gives a wrong answer. How do you debug it?

    First check whether the passage that holds the answer was retrieved. If not, it is a retrieval problem: chunking, the retriever, the pool size or the query. If it was, it is a reading problem: the prompt, the order of passages, or the model. Count the two separately across many questions; in BrewSLM's Pub 17 run, 4 of 9 wrong answers were retrieval misses and 5 were misreads (IRS Pub 17 post, what is RAG).

    Beyond these questions

    Two habits make most of these answers stronger. First, separate retrieval from reading: log whether the passage that answers the question was retrieved, and score the two failures apart. Second, compare on the same questions with a paired test, and say "suggestive" when the test can't separate the arms. Both are cheap and both come up in follow-up questions.
