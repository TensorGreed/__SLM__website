    Embeddings interview questions, with answers you can check

    These questions test whether you have worked with embeddings rather than only called an API: what the vectors are, how to compare and store them, how to pick or train a model, and where they mislead. Each answer is short enough to say out loud and links to a page where the claim is measured with code you can run on a CPU, on the same IRS Publication 17 corpus and 80 frozen questions.

    How to use this page

    Read the question, answer it aloud, then open the answer. Good answers name the trade-off and one thing you would measure. Numbers quoted here come from pages in this track and the RAG track that ran the code on the same corpus: IRS Publication 17 (2025), 2,170 chunks of up to 512 characters, and 80 questions whose answers are verbatim spans of the publication. They are one document and a few small open models, so treat them as an example of how to measure, not as constants for your data.

    The order runs from what an embedding is, through comparing and storing vectors, to choosing, training and using them for data work.

    Questions

    1. What is a text embedding?

    A fixed-length vector a model produces for a text, trained so that texts that belong together (a question and its answer, two paraphrases) point in similar directions. Individual numbers mean nothing; only comparisons between vectors from the same model do. On 80 Pub 17 questions, all-MiniLM-L6-v2 put 71 nearest their own answer span out of 80 candidates (what is an embedding).

    2. How is a sentence embedding produced from a transformer?

    The transformer outputs one vector per word piece; a pooling step combines them into one, usually the mean over non-padding tokens, sometimes the first (CLS) token's vector; many models then normalise to length 1. Printing a sentence-transformers model shows these modules (sentence-transformers basics).

    3. How are embedding models trained?

    Mostly with a contrastive loss on pairs: each anchor should score its own positive higher than every other text in the batch, which act as negatives (in-batch negatives, multiple-negatives ranking loss). Larger batches give more negatives; hard negatives, texts that look similar but are wrong, sharpen it further. The fine-tuning page writes that loss in a few lines of PyTorch.

    4. Cosine similarity, dot product or Euclidean distance?

    Use what the model was trained with. On normalised vectors all three rank identically, because the dot product equals the cosine and L2² = 2 − 2·cos. They differ only when lengths vary: tas-b, a dot-product model, found 66 of 80 Pub 17 answers with the dot product and 62 after normalising, in the expected direction though not significant on 80 questions (cosine, dot and L2).

    5. When should you normalise embeddings?

    When the model was trained for cosine, when your index only offers inner product or L2 and you want cosine ranking, and before k-means or fixed similarity thresholds. Not for a model trained for the dot product, where length carries signal. Do the same on the query and document side (cosine, dot and L2).

    6. What does max_seq_length do, and why does it matter?

    It is the number of word pieces the model reads; the rest of the text is dropped without warning. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 reads 256. With 1,024-character Pub 17 chunks, cutting the limit to 128 dropped top-5 retrieval from 64 to 51 of 80, because 33 answers started after word piece 128 (sentence-transformers basics).

    7. How would you choose an embedding model for a new project?

    Shortlist a few open models by licence, size, input length and language, then measure them on a few dozen of your own labelled queries: recall at the k you will use, MRR, and encoding speed on your hardware. Public leaderboards are a starting point, not an answer. On Pub 17, six open models found between 57 and 69 of 80 answers in their top 5; the popular all-MiniLM-L6-v2 came last and bge-base-en-v1.5 first (12 gained, 0 lost), at about a quarter of the speed. (choosing a model)

    8. What are query and passage prefixes, and what happens if you forget them?

    Some models (e5, bge) were trained with an instruction or a "query: " / "passage: " prefix and expect it at inference. Leaving it out runs without error and usually costs some quality. On Pub 17, bge-small without its query instruction found 62 of 80 instead of 67 (p = 0.062, suggestive), while e5-small did no worse without its prefixes. Follow the card, and measure both. (choosing a model)

    9. How do you reduce the memory of an embedding index?

    Use fewer bits before fewer dimensions. int8 scalar quantization is 4× smaller and lost nothing on Pub 17 (57 of 80 either way); binary is 32× smaller and lost 3, two of which came back by rescoring the top 50 with float vectors. Cutting MiniLM from 384 to 128 dimensions with PCA lost 3, with plain truncation 7 (dimensions vs quality).

    10. What is a Matryoshka embedding?

    One trained so that its first d dimensions work as a smaller embedding on their own, by applying the training loss at several truncation lengths. It lets you pick the size after training. It doesn't make a model better: on Pub 17 a Matryoshka static model at 384 dimensions found 57 of 80, the same as MiniLM, and fell as fast below 256 (dimensions vs quality).

    11. Bi-encoder or cross-encoder?

    A bi-encoder embeds texts separately, so documents are embedded once and search is a vector lookup; a cross-encoder reads query and document together and scores the pair, more accurately but once per pair. Use the bi-encoder to retrieve and the cross-encoder to rerank a short list: on Pub 17 reranking raised top-1 hits from 40 to 54 of 80 (reranking).

    12. When do dense embeddings lose to keyword search?

    On exact identifiers, codes, names and rare terms, which embeddings blur into their neighbours, and on out-of-domain vocabulary. On Pub 17 BM25 and MiniLM tied at top 5 (56 and 57 of 80) but found different questions; fusing them with RRF found 67 (dense retrieval, hybrid search).

    13. When is fine-tuning an embedding model worth it?

    When your domain's notion of similarity differs from what general models learned, you can build pairs that express it, and you have held-out labelled queries to prove the gain. Try a better off-the-shelf model, hybrid search and reranking first; they are cheaper. On Pub 17, one CPU epoch on 1,290 unlabelled sentence-from-passage pairs moved MiniLM from 57 to 61 of 80 (p = 0.29), a third of what switching to a better stock model gained. (fine-tuning an embedding model)

    14. How do you get training pairs without labels?

    From structure in the documents: a sentence and the rest of its passage (the inverse cloze task), a heading and its section, a title and its body, or questions generated by a language model for each passage. Hold out anything your evaluation uses, or the gain is leakage (fine-tuning an embedding model).

    15. How do you detect near-duplicates with embeddings?

    Hash normalised text to remove exact copies first, then embed, compare pairs (all pairs for small sets, nearest neighbours from an index for large ones) and flag those above a threshold calibrated by reading pairs. On Pub 17, 2 chunks were exact duplicates while 69 pairs scored above 0.98 and 4,604 above 0.92 (clustering and dedup).

    16. Why is a similarity threshold not portable?

    Each model has its own score distribution, and so does each corpus: in a tax guide, unrelated question-span pairs still averaged 0.16 with MiniLM, and templated table rows crowded above 0.95. A threshold that means "duplicate" on one dataset flags thousands of distinct pairs on another (what is an embedding, clustering and dedup).

    17. How would you evaluate a clustering of embeddings?

    Against labels when you have them, with the adjusted Rand index or normalised mutual information, plus a shuffled-label baseline for chance; without labels, by sampling members of each cluster and reading them. On Pub 17, k-means on MiniLM vectors matched the 14 chapters with ARI 0.28, no better than TF-IDF's 0.27 (clustering and dedup).

    18. You switch to a new embedding model. What has to happen to the index?

    Re-embed every document with the new model. Vectors from different models live in different spaces, and comparing them gives meaningless scores without any error. Pin model revisions so the same name can't silently change, and store the model id next to the index (what is an embedding, sentence-transformers basics).

    Beyond these questions

    Most follow-up questions come back to two habits. Measure on your own labelled queries, with a paired comparison, before trusting a leaderboard or a default. And keep the pieces consistent: the same model revision, prefixes and normalisation on both the index side and the query side, rebuilt together when any of them changes.
