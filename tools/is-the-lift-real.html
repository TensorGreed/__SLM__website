    What each number means

    Accuracy and its interval

    A score on n rows is a sample: test on a different n rows and you would get a slightly different number. The 95% interval is the range of true accuracies that fit what you saw. It uses the Wilson score interval, not the textbook p ± 1.96·√(p(1−p)/n), which is too narrow near 0% and 100% and has zero width when every row is right or every row is wrong (Brown, Cai and DasGupta 2001).

    The change and its interval

    The change is the fine-tuned accuracy minus the base accuracy, in points. Its interval is what decides the verdict. With the row-by-row counts it is a paired interval (Newcombe 1998, method 10): it knows that the two models were tested on the same rows, so rows both got right, or both got wrong, add no noise. Without them the tool can only treat the two scores as independent samples (Newcombe's unpaired hybrid score interval), which is wider: two models scored on the same rows almost always agree on most of them.

    The exact McNemar test

    Only the rows where the models disagree carry evidence about which is better. If the two models were equally good, each disagreement would be a coin flip. The p-value is the chance of a split at least as lopsided as yours under that coin flip (an exact binomial test, as McNemar's test is computed for small counts). A small p-value, conventionally under 0.05, says the split is unlikely to be luck. The exact form is a little conservative (Fagerland, Lydersen and Laake 2013). With very few disagreeing rows it is also the honest one: 3 rows all one way give p = 0.25 and 4 give 0.125, however the other rows fall, and it takes at least 6 all one way (p = 0.031) to get under 0.05. The paired interval can already sit above zero on 3 or 4 such rows, so the tool asks both to agree before it calls a lift.

    The verdict

    • Real lift: the whole interval for the change is above zero and, when you give the row-by-row counts, the exact McNemar p is under 0.05.
    • Real drop: the same, below zero.
    • Borderline: add rows: with the row-by-row counts, the interval and the exact test disagree. Usually the interval clears zero on a handful of changed rows while p is 0.125 or more: too few rows changed to rule out luck.
    • Within noise: the interval includes zero. That does not mean "no effect"; it means this many rows cannot tell a gain from a loss.
    • Too few rows: under 5 rows, where BrewSLM also gives no verdict.

    BrewSLM's lift check puts a paired t-interval on the mean per-row change (+1, −1 or 0) instead; the tool shows that interval too, so you can set it next to a BrewSLM run (BrewSLM reads its t value from a rounded table above 30 rows, so the ends can differ in the last digit). On a few dozen rows the two intervals differ by a point or two. The 50-row example in the box below is the same case as the first validation row: its "−3 to +19" is BrewSLM's t-interval, the tool's paired interval is −2.7 to +18.3. On its own the t-interval, like the paired interval, can clear zero on 3 or 4 changed rows all one way; this tool does not call that a lift without the exact test.

    How many rows you need

    The smallest change a test set can reliably show, at 95% confidence and 80% power, is 2.80 × s_d / √n, where s_d is the standard deviation of the per-row change and 2.80 = 1.96 + 0.84. Turned around, a change of Δ needs about rows ≈ (2.80 × s_d / Δ)² paired rows. With 0/1 scores s_d² is about the share of rows that changed minus the squared change, so two models that agree on most rows need far fewer. Without pairing the figure is rows ≈ 2.80² × (p_base(1 − p_base) + p_tuned(1 − p_tuned)) / Δ² per model, usually several times more.

    The habits that go with it

    The same advice the academy gives next to every evaluation lesson; the interval is only as honest as the comparison behind it.

    Small test sets: is the difference real?

    Score both models on the same rows and look at the change row by row (a paired comparison), not just at the two headline numbers. On a small test set one answer moves the score a lot: one row in 20 is 5 points, one in 50 is 2.

    BrewSLM pairs the rows, counts how many got better, worse or stayed the same, and puts a 95% confidence interval (a paired t-interval) around the mean change. If the interval includes zero, the change is within noise: the data cannot tell a gain from a loss. Under 5 paired rows it gives no verdict.

    Worked example: 50 test rows
    Base model22 right (44%)
    Fine-tuned26 right (52%): +8 points
    Row by row6 better, 2 worse, 42 the same
    95% interval−3 to +19 points: within noise
    Visible gapabout 16 points at this size; about 500 rows to see a 5-point change

    Habits that keep a small test honest:

    • Compare like with like: same rows, same answer key, same scoring and decoding settings. BrewSLM's Compare gives no verdict when the answer key, judge or scoring differ.
    • Practice, then final: iterate against the practice answer key and score the final one rarely. A set you consult on every decision ends up fitted to your choices.
    • Small slices are noise: a per-class or per-entity score on a handful of rows swings on one row. Show the row count next to it.
    • Seeds: two or three training seeds show how much runs vary; with that few the interval is wide, so read the spread as a rough sign of the noise, not as a test.

    Further reading: Evan Miller, "Adding Error Bars to Evals" (arXiv 2411.00640).

    Questions

    Why should I pair the rows?

    Because the two models answer most rows the same way. Rows both get right, or both get wrong, say nothing about which model is better, and the paired interval leaves them out of the noise. On the same counts it is usually much narrower than the unpaired one, so the same test set can show a smaller real gain.

    Where do I get the row-by-row counts?

    From the per-row scores of both runs: count the rows only the fine-tuned model got right and the rows only the base model got right. BrewSLM's Compare tab and lift check report them as better and worse rows; in your own script, zip the two lists of 0/1 scores and count.

    Does "within noise" mean the fine-tune did nothing?

    No. It means this many rows cannot tell a gain from a loss. The gain may be real and too small to see at this size. The sizing line says how many rows it would take; adding rows, or checking across training seeds, is the way to a clearer answer.

    The interval and the McNemar p-value disagree. Which do I trust?

    Neither on its own: the tool calls a lift or a drop only when both agree. They answer the same question in two slightly different ways, and the exact test is a little conservative. They disagree in two places: near p = 0.05, and when very few rows changed, where the interval can clear zero on 3 or 4 rows all one way while the exact p is 0.125 or more. The tool calls both "borderline" and asks for more rows; don't pick the answer you prefer.

    Does a real lift mean the model is ready to ship?

    No. A real lift says the fine-tune is better than the base model on this test set. Whether it is good enough is a separate rule you set before looking: a pass mark on the metric, per-class floors, no regressions on the cases that matter. Check that the test rows were never trained on, too.

    Can I use this for F1, judge scores or other metrics?

    Not directly: it assumes each row is right or wrong. For a score with partial credit, pair the per-row scores and put a paired t-interval (what BrewSLM's lift check uses) or a paired bootstrap interval on the mean per-row difference.
